# When the Scaffold Stays On: AI, Practice Style, and Screening in Elite Skill Formation 

## Song Yao ∗

## June 3, 2026 

## Abstract 

Generative AI raises short-term productivity by completing tasks that learners would otherwise practice on their own. Whether this substitution erodes frontier skill, the skill required for top-tail non-AI-aided performance, is an open question with growing stakes wherever AI is unavailable or insufficient. The sharper question, however, is whether selection mechanisms can screen apart two coexisting types: substitute-users, who use AI in place of deliberate practice, and complement-users, who use it to accelerate skill development. We exploit a feature of elite programming. The International Collegiate Programming Contest (ICPC) and the International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI) prohibit AI under in-person proctoring and admit entrants through qualification rounds, whereas online Codeforces (CF) contests are unproctored and open to all. From CF submission histories we build an AI-prompt signature, marked by more first-attempt acceptances and fewer attempts and debugging retries, consistent with AI-assisted practice. Three patterns triangulate an institutional-screening mechanism. First, CF practice has shifted toward this signature across entry cohorts spanning two AI rollouts. Second, in open CF contests a stronger signature predicts smaller CF rating gains for users with no ICPC or IOI affiliation, but not for users who qualified for those AI-prohibited contests. Third, inside the AI-prohibited ICPC environment, a shift toward this AI-style practice predicts higher non-AI-aided scores for AI-era entrants. The same practice input thus carries opposite signs depending on whether the contest environment screens for it. The contrast points to two levers: how AI is integrated into training, since within the screened pool AI-style practice coincides with stronger non-AI-aided performance; and the design of AI-prohibited evaluation gates as a type-separating institution. Both extend beyond competitive programming to credentialing systems, from medical and legal boards to professional certification, that must certify skill in a workforce whose practice is increasingly shaped by AI. 

Keywords: generative AI; human capital; skill formation; frontier skill; substitutes and complements; screening; selection mechanisms; credentialing; competitive programming.

# 1 Introduction 

Generative AI can raise short-term output by handling work that learners would otherwise do on their own. Whether that substitution reduces learners’ investment in deep, hard-won human capital, the kind required for top-tail non-AI-aided performance, is a question with growing stakes, especially for frontier work that requires creativity and ability beyond what AI can provide, or where AI is unavailable. We reframe the question on two margins. First, does AI exposure during training predict differential later performance in AI-aided versus AI-prohibited settings? Second, can selection mechanisms distinguish learners who use AI as a substitute for deliberate practice from those who use it to enhance their skill development? Together these two questions are sharper than “does AI affect skill?” For example, calculators make many forms of skill loss look benign: if a tool is always available and reliable, the underlying capability matters less. That argument applies to routine skill but breaks at the frontier. AI fails on genuinely novel problems by design (it recombines patterns from its training data); catching AI errors requires non-AI-aided expertise; trusted institutions such as peer review, medical diagnosis, judicial reasoning, and scientific reproducibility need people who can function without AI mediation; and the innovation pipeline that feeds future training data depends on humans pushing the frontier. Frontier skill is what AI cannot substitute for. By displacing the deliberate practice required for human capital accumulation, AI puts that very frontier skill under pressure. The mechanism mirrors, at the individual level, the macro substitution dynamic in Acemoglu et al. (2026): AI substitutes for human effort and weakens the human-capital investment that produces frontier capability. AI affects skill formation by changing how learners practice. That change in practice effort is what we aim to measure, rather than noise we set aside. We frame the analysis as an extension of standard dynamic skill-formation models (Cunha et al. 2010, Becker 1964, Mincer 1974) in which AI exposure differentially affects practice effort across two coexisting subpopulations: substitute-users , who let the AI do the work and whose underlying skill therefore fails to develop, and complement-users , who use AI as a learning accelerator while continuing to invest in human capital through deliberate practice. The substitute-versus-complement distinction reflects a broader pattern: the same technology produces heterogeneous usage patterns across people with identical access, and the design of credentialing institutions in the presence of such heterogeneity is a mechanism-design problem with crucial socioeconomic stakes. Our empirical strategy in Section 5 identifies this heterogeneity by exploiting the selection structure of AI-prohibited contests: AI-prohibited qualification gates filter out substitute-users, leaving complement-users in the elite pool. We test these ideas in elite competitive programming, a setting that pairs an open, AI-permissive training platform with AI-prohibited contests. Codeforces (CF) is an online platform open to all :2its contests have no qualification filter, so the pool blends substitute-users and complement-users, and AI use during practice is unregulated. Individual CF practice submission histories provide a long panel of submission-level detail on how users practice; CF contest outcomes, on the other hand, provide continuous individual performance measurements. The International Collegiate Programming Contest (ICPC) and the International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI) sit at the other extreme: both prohibit AI and run qualification rounds that screen entrants before the finals (ICPC through peer screening at team formation and team qualifiers, IOI through national olympiads), so the qualifying pool is filtered toward complement-users. Our panel follows 10,419 CF users across two AI rollouts, the GitHub Copilot launch in 2021 and the ChatGPT release in 2022; a subset of these users are linked to ICPC and IOI rosters and contest scores. Three patterns emerge from the analyses on these users, and they differ in how much identifying weight they carry. We present them in that order: a descriptive shift in practice norms across entry cohorts, a suggestive but bounded cross-subpopulation asymmetry, and a robust within-contest association that anchors the paper’s central claim. 

A shift in practice norms across entry cohorts (Prediction 1). Across the platform, the CF practice-mode submissions of later entry cohorts lean toward a behavioral signature: more problems solved on the first attempt, fewer attempts per solved problem, and fewer rapid debugging retries. We draw three lessons, leading with the one the evidence supports most directly. First, AI-prohibited qualification gates act as a population-level filter that separates substitute-style from complement-style practice, sharpening the classical signaling and screening framework of credentials (Spence 1973, Stiglitz 1975) for a setting in which the underlying skill is itself partially substitutable by AI. The opposite-signs pattern across screened and unscreened environments is the evidence for this filtering. Second, the relevant object is how learners use the tool rather than whether they can access it: the same practice input predicts performance loss in the open pool and performance gain inside the screened pool. Third, the findings point to two policy levers. The evidence most directly supports gate design: AI-prohibited evaluation gates at credential boundaries, already standard at elite programming contests. The framework motivates a second lever, redesigning training to preserve non-AI-aided practice alongside AI. Whether either lever generalizes to other elite credentials depends on how far AI substitutes for skill-building practice in those domains. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. This paper sits at the intersection of five literatures. First, macroeconomic theory provides a mechanism of human capital depreciation when AI substitutes for human effort. We extend the framework by distinguishing substitute-users from complement-users of AI and test the resulting predictions at the individual level. Second, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on AI-assisted learning establish the short-term substitution mechanism; the question is whether the mechanism persists in long-term skill formation. Third, workplace and educational observational studies document how AI changes practice and production patterns at scale; we extend those patterns into an elite skill market with a multi-year time window. Fourth, pre-generative-AI evidence from chess training engines shows distributional compression — lower-tail player ratings rise while the world-champion rating stays flat — under complement-style tool use; the open question is whether the elite frontier erodes when the AI can do the work itself, which the chess setting does not test. Finally, we frame AI-prohibited credentialing as a mechanism-design problem, drawing on signaling-and-screening theory in economics. We discuss each stream in turn. 

## 2.1 Macroeconomic theory of AI and human capital 

Acemoglu et al. (2026) provide a dynamic theoretical model in which agentic AI substitutes for human effort, weakening the flow of new knowledge people could contribute to society’s general knowledge stock. Consequently, general knowledge depreciates. Furthermore, under certain conditions, if AI accuracy rises above a critical threshold, complete knowledge collapse becomes the unique stable outcome because people stop investing any effort and delegate work to AI. The model is macro-level, theoretical, and assumes symmetric agents. We test this depreciation mechanism empirically at the individual frontier level, and relax their symmetry assumption: AI exposure differentially affects practice effort across substitute-user and complement-user subpopulations, with the heterogeneity identified through selection into AI-prohibited contests (Section 2.5). Together these provide observational evidence of AI’s effects on long-run skill formation in an elite skill market. 

## 2.2 Substitution mechanism evidence from RCTs on AI-assisted learning 

Recent RCTs have tested AI’s substitution-for-effort mechanism in short-horizon settings. Stadler et al. (2024) find that students using ChatGPT for a 20-minute research task expended about 35% less germane cognitive load (the deep-processing facet that builds understanding) than students using Google search, and produced worse output as a result. Melumad and Yun (2025) show across seven experiments that learning from LLM summaries yields shallower knowledge than learning from web 5search, because the model performs the synthesis the user would otherwise do themselves. Closest to our design, Shen and Tamkin (2026) randomized 52 software developers learning a new Python library: those with high-engagement patterns (asking conceptual questions, requesting explanations alongside generated code) matched or exceeded non-AI users on an unaided follow-up quiz, while those with low-engagement patterns (debug delegation) scored lower. Our AI-prompt signature on CF submissions is the multi-year, field-observational analog of their low-engagement pattern. Bastani et al. (2025) provide the cleanest existing test of substitution-erosion: their field RCT randomizes AI access across 1000 students during mathematics practice and then evaluates their performance on AI-free in-class exams immediately after each practice session. The students were randomized across three arms (Control with no AI access, “GPT Base” directly giving answers to questions, “GPT Tutor” giving hints for solving questions). GPT Base raised practice grades by 48% but reduced exam grades by 17%; GPT Tutor raised practice grades by 127% without harming exam grades. In short, because GPT Base students use AI as substitute for problem-solving during practice, their higher practice grades did not carry through to the AI-free exam. These RCTs establish substitution at the session and task scale; whether it persists is our question. Four features separate our setting. Bastani et al. (2025) run four 90-minute sessions, whereas we follow contestants across multiple years (2018–2025); they estimate mean effects in high-school mathematics, whereas we estimate top-tail effects in elite competitive programming; their AI-prohibited test is a high school course exam, whereas ours are career-defining contests (CF, ICPC, IOI) feeding university admissions and employer signals; and they study students working below the frontier, whereas we study the formative-to-elite pathway in a domain where the frontier is what AI is least able to substitute for. We test the multi-year, top-tail, real-stakes joint cell these dimensions define. 

## 2.3 Workplace and observational AI studies 

A growing observational literature documents how AI tools change practice patterns in workplaces and learning environments. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) document heterogeneous productivity gains in a call-center setting consistent with distributional compression: AI raises mean output, with lower-skilled workers gaining more than higher-skilled ones. Hoffmann et al. (2024) show that GitHub Copilot shifts developers’ time toward core coding and away from project management, with larger effects for lower-ability developers. Shihab et al. (2025) document a programming workflow shift from read → understand → implement to prompt → view response →

implement under Copilot, with manual code entry dropping from 95.6% to 52.5%. Hausman et al. (2025) show that ChatGPT raises university grades, especially for lower-performing students, compressing the grade distribution and eroding grades’ signal value for employers. 6These studies show that AI changes how people work and learn. Their scale is session-level or within-firm; the time window is months. We extend the inquiry to multi-year individual horizons and elite-frontier outcomes. The 2018-2025 CF panel follows individual contestants across two major AI rollouts, and the subpopulation who also appear in ICPC and IOI rosters links their AI-permitted practice to AI-prohibited contest performance. 

## 2.4 Pre-generative-AI evidence: chess engines and why they differ from AI in programming 

Gaessler and Piezunka (2023) document that pre-generative-AI computer-assisted training in chess raised players’ annual Elo ratings (a relative skill rating based on chess game outcomes) by approximately 11 points per year on average. The effect concentrated in lower-skilled players, with world-champion-level Elo being flat during the same period. The chess engines’ impact is therefore a population-level distributional compression: the lower tail rises, the frontier holds steady. This positive Elo result is the most natural objection to our framing. Computer engines have been part of chess training for decades, raising the lower tail while leaving the frontier intact; if the tool did not erode the chess frontier, why would AI-assisted training in programming threaten deep-skill formation at all? The answer turns on three concrete differences between the chess-engine training regime and current AI-programming practice. Each difference moves chess closer to a complement-style integration and AI-programming closer to a substitute-style integration. 1. Complement versus substitute. A chess player consults an engine as an oracle during analysis: it answers “what is the best move here?”, but the player still has to internalize why , and the engine never plays the game. The engine’s output is feedback, not output. AI for programming integrates into the work itself — prompt, generate, accept or modify — and can deliver functioning code without the user grasping the algorithm. Stadler et al. (2024) measure the difference at the cognitive level: AI use suppresses germane cognitive load (the deep processing that builds skill) by 35%. Chess engines do not produce comparable suppression because they do not substitute for that processing. 2. Training-test separation. Competitive chess maintains a hard institutional boundary: engines are forbidden during competition, so players know precisely when they are using the tool and when they are competing unaided. Everyday programming has no equivalent boundary; AI is integrated continuously, with no natural moment that says “stop using AI now.” Bastani et al. (2025) operationalize the distinction: GPT Tutor (scaffolded, no full solutions) did not harm unaided performance, while GPT Base (continuous full-solution access) cut unaided exam scores by 17% — same domain, same students, scaffolded versus unscaffolded use. AI-prohibited 7contests (Section 2.5) reinstate the chess-style separation that everyday programming lacks, and that separation is the feature our research design exploits. 3. What the tool can substitute for. A chess engine is a powerful study aid, but unaided games assess runtime cognition the engine cannot perform in the moment: pattern recognition under time pressure, calculation, strategic planning, composure. There is a gap between what the engine does (analysis between games) and what competition measures (live play). AI in programming substitutes for design-time cognition — algorithm design, debugging, problem decomposition — which is precisely what programming contests measure. There is no such gap: the AI does the very thing the contestant is scored on. Read together, these differences make the positive Elo result of Gaessler and Piezunka (2023) consistent with — not against — our framing: chess engines raised ratings through a complement-style mode of use, whereas current programming practice sits closer to the substitute side. Whether this substitute-style risk shows up in an elite skill market over multi-year horizons is the question this paper tests. 

## 2.5 Selection into AI-prohibited credentialing 

The previous four streams establish what AI does to skill formation through substitution mechanisms and what it does to practice patterns at workplace scale. The fifth question motivates our research design: can evaluation institutions screen productive from unproductive AI use when the technology partially substitutes for the skill being evaluated? The distinction we draw is between substitute-users and complement-users of AI: contestants with the same AI access during practice differ in whether they use it as a substitute for deliberate practice or as a complement, and that difference accumulates into divergent non-AI-aided performance. This is the multi-year, field-scale analog of the engagement-pattern heterogeneity in the RCT evidence (Section 2.2): the same tool yields different skill outcomes depending on how the user engages it. The evaluation gate filters through two channels at once: formal rules (no AI, no internet, in-person, supervised) and social selection (peer screening at team formation, national-olympiad reputation), which together determine who reaches the elite qualifying pool. The design question is not whether to permit AI but how to make the gate filter on the substitute-vs-complement margin. Classical signaling (Spence 1973) and screening (Stiglitz 1975) theory treat credentials as costly actions or tests that separate types; our setting differs in that the effort is genuinely skill-forming rather than purely type-sorting, and the institution actively shapes which separating equilibrium obtains by designing the gate. What is new with generative AI is that the deep skill is now partially substitutable by AI, so the gate’s filtering structure, not just its existence, determines whether the credential still separates types. 8We make three contributions to this stream: observational evidence at multi-year individual scale that the substitute-vs-complement distinction is empirically real and accumulates into divergent non-AI-aided performance; identification of a selection mechanism, in which ICPC’s peer screening at team formation and IOI’s national-olympiad filter screen substitute-users out before the contest, which we treat as a research-design feature rather than a sample-selection nuisance; and a sharpened policy claim that gate design should target the substitute-vs-complement margin rather than rely on generic gatekeeping. Together these position the contribution as a design principle for evaluation systems in the presence of partially substitutable AI. 

# 3 Background and Empirical Setting 

## 3.1 The AI shocks 

GitHub Copilot entered technical preview on June 29, 2021 and reached general availability one year later. It targeted programming directly and was among the first AI tools widely adopted by programmers. ChatGPT followed on November 30, 2022, putting generative AI into broad public use across populations and tasks. Subsequent releases (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 2 and 3, DeepSeek-Coder) further extended AI’s coding capability but did not introduce a comparably sharp adoption shock. The two tools also embody different functional affordances (Markus and Silver 2008), the possibilities for action a technology offers its users. The in-editor autocomplete of Copilot affords inline, line-level code suggestion that a programmer accepts or edits while typing; the conversational interface of ChatGPT affords whole-solution generation, natural-language explanation, and iterative debugging through prompting. These differing affordances shape how AI is enacted in routine practice (Orlikowski 2000), and they help interpret why the quick-retry margin shifts in opposite directions around the two releases (Section 5.1; Appendix A.2). The two releases play different roles in our research design. Copilot was a programmer-specific tool and arrived more than a year before ChatGPT, so users who first appeared on CF after the Copilot release had already begun training with AI tools available to them. ChatGPT was a broader population-level event with a sharper date, observable across the entire panel rather than only among the early adopters of Copilot. We use the Copilot date to partition users by training vintage, and the ChatGPT date to define the within-user pre/post comparison; we elaborate on the motivation for this split in Section 5. 93.2 Competitive programming as the empirical setting 

CF is the dominant online competitive programming platform. It has run contests several times a week since 2010. It issues individual ratings based on each user’s performance in the contests compared to others. The CF rating is Elo-style, like chess, measuring each user’s relative skill. Besides weekly contests, anyone can create an account and submit practice solutions. Practice submissions do not enter the rating system and are a good training sandbox. There is no qualification gate, no proctoring during practice, and nothing preventing a user from running an AI tool while solving problems. The platform is, in this sense, an open pool during the practice phase: the participating population mixes substitute-users with complement-users, and the platform does nothing to separate them. During rated contests AI use is again hard to police, but AI may be a weaker substitute there: under time limits and a point penalty for incorrect submissions, contestants who lean on AI without the underlying skill tend to lose rating rather than gain it (see Section 5). The International Collegiate Programming Contest (ICPC) and the International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI) sit on the other end of the institutional spectrum. Both run multi-stage qualification rounds before the finals, and both prohibit AI tools at every stage. ICPC builds teams of three from a single university and holds its AI-prohibited qualification rounds and World Finals in person. IOI selects individual contestants through national olympiads and runs the international round in person (online only during 2020 and 2021 due to COVID-19). Users whose CF performance leans heavily on AI are unlikely to pass these rounds, so the populations that reach the ICPC finals and the IOI delegations have been screened of the most AI-dependent practitioners. These are high-stakes contests whose medals are recruiting signals and are cited on university applications, so the non-AI-aided scores are a meaningful frontier measure rather than a lab proxy. The contrast between the more AI-lenient CF pool and the screened ICPC and IOI pools is what lets us identify the substitution mechanism. The CF pool keeps both user types visible and shows how AI exposure correlates with rating change in an unscreened population. The qualified ICPC and IOI pools have been pre-filtered by an AI-prohibited gate and show how AI-intensive practice translates into non-AI-aided performance among the survivors of that filter. Reading the two together gives cross-environment evidence on the same training inputs. On the CF platform, one further feature matters for our empirical strategy. Each submission carries a tag indicating whether it is in practice or contest mode. The behavioral signature of AI-style practice is cleanest in practice mode, where substitution operates freely and the data are dense, so we use the practice-mode signature as our main measure of what we term AI-intensity :a practice-style proxy for how far a user’s practice has shifted toward an AI-style prompt-and-fix workflow, although not a verified measure of AI use. The same signature computed on contest-mode submissions, noisier because contest behavior also reflects contest skill and time pressure, provides a robustness check against a roster-versus-skill confound (Appendix A.3): the cross-subpopulation 10 pattern it shows is consistent with the practice-mode measure. 

## 3.3 Testable predictions 

The setting described above pairs an open, AI-permissive practice environment (CF) with AI-prohibited contests (ICPC and IOI) whose qualification rounds screen entrants. Two features of this structure generate the predictions that the rest of the paper tests. The first is the substitute-versus-complement distinction of Section 2.5: users with the same AI access during practice differ in whether they let the tool do the work, so that their non-AI-aided skill does not develop (substitute-users), or use it as a learning accelerator while continuing to invest in deliberate practice (complement-users). The second is selection: because an AI-prohibited gate cannot be passed on substituted skill, it filters substitute-users out of the qualified pool, leaving a screened population tilted toward complement-users. These two features yield three predictions, which Section 5 tests in turn. 

Prediction 1 (universal practice shift). After the AI releases, CF practice behavior shifts toward an AI-prompt signature — more first-attempt acceptances, fewer attempts per solved problem, and fewer rapid debugging retries — across the platform as a whole, and the shift grows with how much of a user’s training postdates AI. This prediction concerns practice inputs and does not by itself separate substitution from complementarity. 

Prediction 2 (asymmetry in the un-screened pool). In the open CF pool, where both user types coexist, a stronger AI-prompt signature predicts smaller rating gains among un-screened (non-rostered) users, because substitute-users remain in this pool and their non-AI-aided skill erodes. Among roster-linked users, who have passed AI-prohibited gates, the same signature does not predict rating loss, because the gate has already removed the substitute-users. The signature-to-rating slope is therefore more negative in the un-screened subpopulation than in the screened one. 

Prediction 3 (positive slope inside the screened pool). Inside the AI-prohibited ICPC environment, among users who entered competitive programming in the AI era, a larger shift toward AI-intensive practice predicts higher non-AI-aided contest score. Once the gate has screened substitute-users out, the AI-style practice that remains reflects complement use, which builds frontier skill rather than eroding it. Predictions 2 and 3 together form the sharpest test. The same training input, a shift toward AI-style practice, is predicted to carry opposite signs depending on whether the contest environment screens substitute-users out: negative in the un-screened CF pool (Prediction 2) and positive inside the screened ICPC pool (Prediction 3). A single mechanism operating uniformly, whether pure erosion or pure enhancement, cannot produce both signs; only the combination of heterogeneous use and selective screening does. 11 4 Data

## 4.3 Outcome measurement: scoring system properties 

Each contest has its own scoring system, so our outcome variable differs by contest; we discuss each choice in turn. The CF rating is a modified, self-calibrating Elo-rating system inspired by chess (Mirzayanov 2015). After each contest, a competitor’s rating moves up or down according to how they placed against the others in that round, and the ratings of those opponents enter the calculation directly. The rating therefore already reflects the strength of the field a competitor faced, rather than something we must control for separately. CF also applies an inflation correction that keeps the rating scale roughly stable from year to year, so a rating change of a given size means about the same thing across years. The rating stays relative (a contestant’s standing in the pool, not an absolute skill level), but our CF analyses compare a contestant with their own past and compare subpopulations within the same window, so a common shift in the pool’s level nets out. In contrast, ICPC and IOI provide no comparable self-calibrating rating. Rather than fall back on the contest rank or medal, a relative outcome that depends on the then-current contestant pool, our primary outcomes are the absolute scores: problems solved at the ICPC World Finals, and total points at the IOI. A score does not depend on the contestant pool at all; it reflects a competitor’s own performance on a fixed problem set. It does, however, vary with the difficulty of each year’s problem set. Accordingly, we include contest-year fixed effects to absorb this variation.

This section presents three sets of analyses that jointly distinguish the substitute and complementary roles of generative AI in skill formation; they test Predictions 1–3 of Section 3.3, respectively. The first set (Section 5.1) documents a cross-cohort shift in CF practice patterns toward an AI-prompt signature, larger for cohorts more of whose training postdates AI; we read this shift as descriptive context, because the cohort contrast is not cleanly timed to the AI releases. The second (Section 5.2) shows a cross-subpopulation asymmetry in CF users’ ratings: this same AI-prompt signature predicts rating loss among users in the open CF user pool, but not among rostered users who are linked to AI-prohibited contests, ICPC and IOI. The third (Section 5.3) moves inside the AI-prohibited ICPC environment and finds that AI-intensive CF practice predicts higher non-AI-aided scores for AI era entrants, resolving the asymmetry puzzle: the team formation and qualifier screen substitute-users out, leaving a pool in which the remaining AI use reflects AI-complement practice that enhances frontier skill formation. Section 5.4 discusses one robustness consideration, COVID-19’s impact on the results, with the full battery in Appendix A. All three analyses share the cohort structure of Section 4.2 (Table 2), with the pre AI cohort as the reference. The cohort cutoff is anchored at the Copilot launch and the within-user pre/post comparisons at the later ChatGPT release, for the reasons set out in Section 5.1. 

## 5.1 The behavior shift toward an AI-prompt signature in CF practice 

We first evaluate how CF practice behavior shifts after the AI releases. Specifically, we focus on three patterns that together form an AI-prompt signature: 1. frac single shot : the fraction of problems solved on the first attempt; 2. median attempts : the median number of attempts to solve a problem; 3. frac quick retry : the fraction of quick retries after a failed submission, where a quick retry is defined as a reattempt within thirty minutes of the incorrect submission. Solving a problem without AI is iterative: the user submits, reads the verdict, locates the bug, and resubmits, often several times before the solution is accepted as correct (“AC,” the platform’s Accepted verdict). A user who relies on AI-assistance can instead submit a generated solution that often clears the problem on the first try, collapsing the submit-debug-resubmit cycle into a single submission, consistent with the keystroke-level workflow shift documented by Shihab et al. (2025). Consequently, if users use AI during practice, three patterns are likely to surface: 1. more problems solved on the first attempt (greater frac single shot ); 19 2. fewer total submissions per solved problem (lower median attempts ); and 3. fewer quick retries after a failed submission (lower frac quick retry ). Fewer quick retries are the subtlest of the three. Quick retries signal rapid human debugging, where the programmer submits, identifies the bug in code they wrote, and resubmits within a short time span. AI reliance reduces quick retries on two fronts: more first submissions succeed and leave nothing to retry, and, more importantly, when an AI-generated solution does fail, the user often cannot quickly debug code that they did not write themselves. Throughout, this signature is a behavioral proxy inferred from submission timing. It is consistent with AI-style practice, but not a direct, verified measure of AI use. Appendix B reports construct-validation tests that bound the leading alternative: the signature merely proxies programmer skill because more skilled programmers may show similar behavior patterns even without AI. Specifically, we show that, before AI was available, higher-rated (more skilled) users were, if anything, less AI-like on these behaviors, not more (the correlation between CF rating and each signature component is weak, |𝑟 | < 0.20, and runs opposite to the AI direction), so skill does not produce the signature. The choice is also substantively defensible. ChatGPT was the broader shock: free, web-based, and immediately mainstream, while Copilot in 2021 was a programmer-specific tool with a much narrower user base. The practice-pattern signature shift visible in the data is also larger around the ChatGPT date than around the Copilot date. ## 5.4 Robustness 

COVID disruption (2020–2021). The empirical window straddles the COVID-19 pandemic. ICPC 2020 was postponed and held in 2021 with reduced attendance; IOI 2020 was held virtually with reduced participation; multiple national qualification pipelines were disrupted across 2020 and 2021. The CF practice panel is the most COVID-resistant of the three samples because it is continuous rather than tied to an annual contest schedule; the cohort × post DiD on practice-pattern proxies (Section 5.1) absorbs year-level shocks through its quarter fixed effects, and within-user rating dynamics include 2020 and 2021 observations as part of the natural trajectory rather than as anomalies to be dummied out. The cross-subpopulation CF rating asymmetry (Section 5.2) is similarly COVID-resistant: the rating-change outcome Δ𝑟 ℎ is constructed as a pre-ChatGPT-versus-post-ChatGPT difference where both windows span multiple years, washing out year-specific shocks. The within-ICPC AI-intensity test (Section 5.3) restricts to contest years ≥ 2023 , which entirely post-dates COVID, so the COVID years cannot directly contaminate the AI-intensity coefficient. Where a specification is not structurally immune in this way, namely the event studies, which estimate a coefficient for each calendar year, we make an explicit adjustment rather than rely on the design: we set the reference period to 2019 (the last pre-COVID year) instead of the COVID-adjacent 2022, and absorb 2020 and 2021 with their own dummies. 33 Broader robustness battery. # 6 Discussion 

The findings combine into one internally consistent picture: a cross-cohort shift in practice patterns toward an AI prompt-and-fix style, which we read as descriptive; a cross-subpopulation asymmetry in which the AI-prompt signature predicts CF rating loss in the un-screened, non-rostered pool but not in the screened, roster-linked pool, bounded by differential attrition; and a robust positive within-ICPC association between a shift toward AI-style practice and non-AI-aided contest score among AI-era entrants who survived the qualification gate. These pieces fit together only under the selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests structure, which is the central contribution of this paper. 

## 6.1 Theoretical contributions 

This paper makes three theoretical contributions to the literature on AI and skill formation. First, the paper introduces the selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests structure as a load-bearing feature of empirical designs in this literature. Competitive credentialing systems with AI-prohibited gates filter substitute-dependent agents out of the elite pool. This filtering changes which signals appear in which observational windows: the substitution-driven rating signal from substitute-users is observable only on AI-allowed platforms, while AI-prohibited contests, having screened those users out, reveal only the complement-user association among the deep-skill subpopulation that remains. Recognizing this selection structure reconciles the cross-subpopulation and within-ICPC findings that would otherwise appear contradictory, and it has implications for any empirical design that uses credentialing-gate variation to identify AI substitution effects. This first contribution is, more specifically, a contribution to information systems. The AI-prohibited evaluation gate is a designed IT-enabled institution, not merely an economic screening device: its rules (no AI, in-person, proctored) and its qualification structure are design choices that determine whether differential AI use is rendered visible or screened out. The AI-prompt signature, in turn, is a measure of technology-in-practice (Orlikowski 2000) rather than of technology adoption: it captures how AI is enacted in routine work, not whether a user can access it. Our central result, that the same enacted practice carries opposite performance signals depending on the screening design of the evaluation environment in which skill is later tested, is therefore a statement about sociotechnical evaluation-system design. A purely labor-economics reading would frame the same 34 evidence as human-capital depreciation; the information-systems reading locates the policy lever in the design of the evaluation artifact, which is where institutions can act. Second, the paper operationalizes the mechanism of human capital depreciation in Acemoglu et al. (2026) at the individual frontier scale, providing a multi-year, field-observational counterpart to the macro-theoretical argument and the laboratory evidence in Bastani et al. (2025), Stadler et al. (2024), and Shen and Tamkin (2026). The cross-cohort practice-pattern shift we document, toward an AI prompt-and-fix submission style at multi-year scale, is consistent with the debug-skipping mechanism that Shen and Tamkin (2026) identify in an RCT. Third, the paper raises broader implications for the value of frontier skill in an AI-augmented world. Five considerations support continued investment in non-AI-aided capability even as AI tools become ubiquitous: 1. AI failure modes and verification. AI hallucinates, fails on novel problems by design (it is recombination over the training distribution), and produces plausible-looking output that is subtly wrong. Catching AI errors requires non-AI-aided expertise. Bastani et al. (2025) found exactly this: GPT-Base students performed 17% worse on non-AI-aided exams and did not perceive they were performing worse . Self-perception is unreliable; a workforce dependent on AI but lacking non-AI-aided skill to verify AI output cannot detect propagating errors. 2. Frontier creation, not just frontier execution. AI is a powerful interpolator across its training distribution but a poor extrapolator beyond it. Genuine breakthroughs — novel theorems, new scientific paradigms, out-of-distribution insights — remain primarily human contributions, even as AI accelerates surrounding execution. Acemoglu et al. (2026) formalizes this: if humans stop generating outside-distribution ideas, the training distribution stagnates and AI itself ceases to improve. 3. Critical windows in skill formation. Stadler et al. (2024)’s mediation result (germane cognitive load fully mediates AI’s effect on output quality) implies that AI substitutes precisely for the deep cognitive engagement that builds schemas. Schemas built early provide the substrate for later expertise; if high-potential entrants fail to build them during their formative years, they may never build them, even if AI is later removed. 4. Welfare resilience under AI-access disruption. AI access can be revoked through subscription cost, political restriction (Italy’s 2023 ChatGPT ban; China’s continual restriction; EU AI Act effects), corporate policy, infrastructure failure, security breach, or geopolitical isolation. Populations dependent on AI lose capability instantly when AI access disappears; populations with non-AI-aided skill retain a fallback. 35 5. Verification in trusted institutions. Courts, peer review, medical diagnosis, scientific reproducibility, and financial auditing rely on humans who can independently verify claims without AI mediation. The verification crisis is downstream of, but distinct from, the production crisis. 

## 6.2 Practical implications and policy levers 

The findings suggest two complementary policy levers for preserving non-AI-aided frontier capability in elite skill markets. They operate at different scales and through different mechanisms, and they reinforce rather than substitute for each other. 

Lever 1: within-training, complement-style AI integration. Our within-gate result makes this lever more than a conjecture. Among survivors of the qualification gate, a stronger shift toward AI-style practice is associated with higher non-AI-aided ICPC performance (Section 5.3): the complement channel, AI that supports rather than supplants skill formation, is visible among elite performers. What our design cannot identify is whether the mode of integration can be steered to put more users on that channel; Bastani et al. (2025)’s classroom RCT supplies the causal step, showing that scaffolded AI (problem-specific hints rather than full solutions) eliminates the substitution effect on non-AI-aided performance. Read together, the field pattern and the RCT motivate a training principle for elite settings: integrate AI as a scaffolded complement rather than a full-solution substitute. 

Lever 2: AI-prohibited evaluation gates at the population level. The selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests structure functions as a population-level filtering mechanism. Programmers whose competitive performance relies heavily on AI cannot pass AI-prohibited qualification rounds; the elite pipeline retains deep-skill investors while substitute-users remain visible only on AI-allowed platforms. The cross-subpopulation asymmetry (Section 5.2) is consistent with the filter screening substitute-users out before the contest. The within-ICPC test (Section 5.3) is consistent with the same filter: the positive within-gate association noted under Lever 1 reflects complement-user selection, the substitute-users having been screened out upstream. The lever is the existence and design of AI-prohibited evaluation moments at the population scale, not the elimination of AI from training. 

Selection vs. anticipation: what we identify and what we do not. The mechanism we identify for Lever 2 is selection : AI-prohibited gates filter substitute-dependent programmers out of the elite pipeline. A separate, complementary mechanism — anticipation — would operate behaviorally: programmers who know the AI scaffold will be removed at critical evaluation moments invest more in deep skill during training and use AI as a complement rather than a substitute. The two mechanisms have different population-level implications. Under selection alone, AI-prohibited 36 gates sort but do not change individual behavior; under anticipation, the gates’ existence shifts the training distribution itself. Our data are consistent with both channels but cannot distinguish them. Identifying the anticipation channel would require a natural experiment in gate-introduction (or removal), or a longitudinal design tracking training-behavior changes after credible AI-prohibition signals. We flag this as productive future work and rest the policy argument here on the selection channel that we identify. This policy argument rests on the screening we document, not on a proven erosion effect. The opposite-signs pattern shows that AI-prohibited gates separate practice types, so the gate is the relevant lever whether or not substitute-style practice ultimately erodes frontier skill; if there is a long-run cost, it concentrates among the substitute-users the gate screens out. The argument is population-level, not within-individual: AI access is privately valuable to most users, and the externality, if any, is at the level of the elite pool from which frontier capability is drawn. 

## 6.3 External validity 

The argument for AI-prohibited evaluation gates has natural limits, and the lesson is not “ban AI from all evaluation.” Three considerations bound the generalization from competitive programming to other elite skill markets. 

Existing gate infrastructure varies by field. Medicine, law, and scientific peer review already have AI-prohibition structures that approximate our setting. Medical board exams (USMLE Step 1–3, internal medicine recertification) are proctored and AI-prohibited; the bar exam similarly restricts AI use during the timed test. Scientific peer review has informal but increasingly explicit AI-prohibition norms at journals and grant agencies. Within these fields, the selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests framework should extend: substitute-users of AI during training would face the same kind of gate-level filter that competitive programmers face at ICPC qualification. Fields without such gates — much of corporate software engineering, marketing analytics, financial services analysis — do not have a comparable filtering mechanism, and the population-level externality would operate without the screening buffer that our setting documents. 

Designing AI-prohibited evaluations is itself difficult. Even where gate structures exist, designing evaluations that effectively prohibit AI use is increasingly hard as AI becomes embedded in evaluation infrastructure (proctoring tools, scoring systems, candidate-side LLM-assisted note-taking on un-proctored portions). Our setting (ICPC, IOI) benefits from on-site proctoring at qualification and final-round stages; many other fields cannot replicate this. The lesson is that AI-prohibited evaluation gates have a filtering function that compounds with within-training safeguards, and removing or weakening them has population-level consequences worth weighing against the productivity and capability gains of AI-permissive evaluation. 37 Different fields face different AI-erosion risks. The competitive programming setting is one where AI tools are unusually effective at the focal task (code generation, debugging, problem decomposition). Fields where AI is currently weaker — novel mathematical proof, surgical decision-making, qualitative interpretation in social science — face a smaller substitution-erosion risk in the near term, though this is a moving target as model capabilities advance. The framework is robust to the substitution rate in any specific field; what varies is the urgency of the policy lever. 

## 6.4 Non-AI alternative explanations 

The alternatives we name below operate across subpopulations, so they bear chiefly on the cross-subpopulation CF rating asymmetry (Prediction 2, Section 5.2). The within-ICPC AI-intensity slope (Prediction 3, Section 5.3) is identified within the screened pool, and the within-user practice-pattern shift (Prediction 1, Section 5.1) within the same users across the AI shock; both are correspondingly less exposed to these channels. 

Tech labor market shocks during 2022–2024. Macroeconomic and tech-sector labor-market shocks over this period may have hit countries with the strongest pre-AI competitive-programming bases asymmetrically: elite programmers there may have moved into industry roles or relocated, depleting the AI-prohibited-contest pipeline. The cross-subpopulation asymmetry could partly capture this depletion rather than direct AI-driven skill erosion. 

Demographic and curricular shifts. Top universities increasingly redirect elite computer-science talent toward applied AI/ML curricula. If elite institutions in those countries pivoted faster, traditional competitive-programming participation may have declined as a side effect rather than direct AI-driven skill erosion. 

Regression to the mean. Year fixed effects absorb the average; the within-subpopulation differential underlying the cross-subpopulation asymmetry is not mechanically driven by mean reversion. But if some subpopulations had unusually strong cohorts going into 2022, modest reversion could amplify the post-2022 differential. The roster-linked subpopulation is defined ex ante by ICPC participation, which makes pure reversion-to-the-mean a plausible alternative to active erosion for the cross-subpopulation specification. For the practice-pattern shift (Prediction 1) we do not claim AI substitution over these alternatives: we treat the cohort AI-style practice result as descriptive rather than causal. The screening argument rests instead on the joint pattern of Predictions 2 and 3, which the alternatives do not naturally produce: labor-market, curricular, or reversion-to-the-mean shifts would not generate a negative cross-subpopulation asymmetry coexisting with a positive within-ICPC slope (non-rostered 𝜃 NR < 0,roster-linked 𝜃 RL ≈ 0, within-ICPC 𝜓 AI > 0). The selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests structure organizes that opposite-signs pattern with a single mechanism, whereas the non-AI alternatives would 38 each have to explain the two signs separately. That said, we cannot fully rule the alternatives out, and the cross-subpopulation asymmetry in particular remains attrition-bounded; our interpretation acknowledges this uncertainty. 

## 6.5 Limitations and future research 

Identification limits. Observational identification has bounded reach. Selection-on-entry into the broad CF population is partially handled by within-individual fixed effects, but selection on continued participation, on AI tool use, and on the choice to enter qualification rounds is not fully eliminated. Pre-screening at country-level olympiad selection gates (which themselves prohibit AI) further attenuates the substitution signal in the linked ICPC/IOI cohort relative to the broader competitive-programming population, making contest-level effects a conservative test. The COVID-aware robustness reported in Section 5.4 defends the primary identification but does not resolve all identification concerns. 

Generalizability beyond competitive programming. Our setting is competitive programming. Extension to other elite skill markets — mathematics, theoretical computer science, software engineering, professional research — requires either domain-specific natural experiments or cross-domain meta-evidence. 

COVID confound. The empirical window straddles the 2020–2021 COVID disruption. Section 5.4 reports COVID-aware sensitivity: the within-individual mechanism evidence and the within-ICPC slope are the cleanest specifications for the AI-shock comparison, the latter because it restricts to contest years 2023 and later, which postdate COVID. 

Measurement of AI use and filter strength. We do not directly observe which CF users adopt which AI tools. The cohort × post DiD identifies practice-pattern shifts at the cohort level; the subpopulation comparison computes a within-user proxy from observed submission-pattern changes. Neither directly measures AI use. The construct-validity case for the AI-prompt signature — discriminant validity against pre-shock skill, and within-user stability around AI release among established users — is developed in Appendix B; Section 5.1 additionally reports that the task-amenability gradient does not discriminate AI from a secular or maturation trend once career stage is held fixed. More fundamentally, the cross-cohort variation in the signature is not identified as AI-caused: the maturation-clean tests in Section 5.1 (the cohort event study, career-time matching, and the difficulty gradient) show a secular practice-norm and novice-maturation component, so the AI reading of the cohort shift is the leading interpretation rather than a demonstrated effect. The selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests framing rests on the empirical claim that qualification rounds effectively screen out substitute-users; we do not observe filter strength directly. Imperfect proctoring or indirect AI benefit through training would weaken the framework. The within-ICPC 39 positive slope partially probes filter strength: the positive sign within the qualifier sample is consistent with effective filtering (the residual variation in AI-intensity after the filter selects on complement-users, whose AI-intensity is associated with higher performance); a strong negative sign would indicate the filter is leaky enough that substitute-users contribute meaningfully to within-sample variation. A platform-level or survey-based user-level AI-use measurement would close both identification gaps and is a priority future-work item. 

Future research directions. Several extensions follow naturally from the design. First, distinguishing the selection channel from the anticipation channel for AI-prohibited evaluation gates would require a natural experiment in gate introduction or removal, or a longitudinal design tracking training-behavior changes after credible AI-prohibition signals. Second, extension to other elite skill markets where AI-prohibited gates exist (e.g., medical board exams, legal bar exams, scientific peer review) would test the generalizability of the selection mechanism. Third, user-level AI-use measurement through platform telemetry or developer surveys would close the identification gap on individual AI use, sharpening the test of substitution at the individual level. Fourth, a future update incorporating ICPC 2026–2027 and IOI 2026–2027 outcomes would extend the post-shock observation window for the AI-era cohort whose effects are most informative for long-run skill formation. 

## 6.6 Conclusion 

We ask whether AI-prohibited evaluation gates can separate two coexisting types of AI practice in elite competitive programming, and we assemble evidence in three patterns. The practice-pattern shift is a cross-cohort move toward the AI-prompt signature on the CF practice panel. The cross-subpopulation asymmetry appears in CF rating gains: among non-rostered CF users (no ICPC/IOI selection), a stronger AI-prompt signature during practice predicts lower contest performance; among roster-linked users (selected through AI-prohibited gates), the relationship becomes null. The within-ICPC result shows that a shift toward AI-style practice predicts higher non-AI-aided performance. The same practice input thus carries opposite signs on contest performance depending on whether the contest environment screens. The unified reading is that AI-prohibited qualification gates separate substitute-style from complement-style practice, filtering substitute-users out before they reach elite contests; the selection-into-AI-prohibited-contests structure is the load-bearing feature of the design, and it makes the cross-environment evidence coherent rather than contradictory. The motivating question of our research is whether short-run AI productivity gains carry a long-run cost to elite human-capital formation; our evidence speaks to the gatekeeping institution that would determine whether the long-run cost reaches the elite skill frontier, not to the existence and size of the cost itself. How AI is used within training is one policy lever; the design of the AI-prohibited 40 evaluation gates that screen access to the elite skill frontier is another. Both levers are worth weighing, as they carry substantial, long-term socioeconomic implications. 41 A Robustness

Roster-skill confound check 

The cross-subpopulation asymmetry could in principle reflect a roster-versus-skill confound: roster-linked users are systematically higher-skill, having qualified for ICPC and IOI rosters through competitive selection, so a story in which higher-skill users have systematically different post-shock rating dynamics could in principle produce the asymmetry without any AI-specific channel. The headline mechanism results (Section 5.1) and the individual-level qualifier-stage test rest on the AI-prompt signature: the behavioral composite of higher single-shot accept rate, lower median attempts-to-AC, and lower quick-retry rate that we attribute to AI prompt-and-fix substitution. A skeptical reading is that this signature captures programmer skill or an analytic work style rather than AI use directly. This appendix bounds that concern with three tests on the Codeforces panel restricted to submissions where author.participantType == "PRACTICE" (the canonical practice filter on the combined re-scraped panel; each test below reports its own sample). The tests address two distinct alternatives to the AI-substitution interpretation. Subsection B.1 tests whether the signature is a proxy for skill, by examining its relationship to CF rating in the strictly pre-shock window (2018-Q1 to 2022-Q3). Subsection B.2 examines whether established practitioners exhibit a within-user discontinuity in the signature at AI release dates, which would distinguish a discrete AI-availability effect from a smooth skill-acquisition trend. Subsection B.3 synthesizes the two tests against the experimental causal anchor from Shen and Tamkin (2026).

## B.3 Synthesis with experimental evidence 

The two tests bound two distinct alternatives to the AI-substitution interpretation. The discriminant test (Subsection B.1) rules out the signature as a proxy for static skill: the cross-sectional sign runs opposite to the AI-prompt prediction and the within-user skill-on-signature elasticity is an order of magnitude too small to explain the Pattern A cohort effect. The event-study test (Subsection B.2) rules out adoption by established practitioners: there is no within-user drift in the signature around the AI releases, which locates the Pattern A cohort effect in cohort composition rather than in existing-user adoption. What these two tests establish is narrower than AI causation: the cross-cohort signature difference is a genuine, cohort-compositional shift in practice style, not an artifact of static skill or of established users adopting AI. They do not establish that the cohort difference is caused by AI. As Section 5.1 shows, the cohort gradient does not coincide with the AI releases (the cohort event study rejects parallel trends and shows no discontinuity), the AI-era and transition cohorts are indistinguishable at matched career stage, and the maturation-clean difficulty gradient is skill-like rather than AI-amenable. The signature is therefore best read as a practice-style proxy whose cross-cohort variation carries a secular and maturation component; the AI interpretation is the leading reading, not an identified effect. Shen and Tamkin (2026)’s randomized experiment on 52 software developers provides the complementary causal anchor. Their RCT randomly assigns AI use during a learning task and finds that AI users score substantially lower on a subsequent non-AI-aided assessment (a 17% reduction, Cohen’s 𝑑 = 0.738 , 𝑝 = 0.010 ), with no significant gain in completion time; the deficit is concentrated among low-engagement, prompt-and-fix usage patterns rather than the cognitively engaged ones. That low-engagement mode of working is the behavioral counterpart to the AI-prompt signature we measure; because AI use is randomly assigned in their design, its link to weaker 54 non-AI-aided performance is causally identified in that setting. Our signature is the multi-year, field-observational analog of their experimentally induced pattern. Taken together, the field tests and the experimental anchor establish that the AI-prompt signature is a behavioral practice-style proxy consistent with AI-assisted practice: the discriminant and event-study tests rule out static skill and existing-user adoption, and Shen and Tamkin (2026)’s RCT shows that AI use produces this very pattern. What they do not establish is that the cross-cohort variation in the signature is AI-caused; the maturation-clean evidence in Section 5.1 attributes part of it to secular practice-norm change and novice maturation. We therefore use the signature as a proxy for AI-style practice throughout, and treat its AI interpretation as the leading but unverified reading rather than a measured fact. 55 0.08

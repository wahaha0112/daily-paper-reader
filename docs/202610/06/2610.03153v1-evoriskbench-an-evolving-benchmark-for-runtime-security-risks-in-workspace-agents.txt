ABSTRACT 

Workspace agents combine large language models with execution harnesses to perform stateful, multi-step tasks that access or modify external resources. Ex-isting benchmarks leave gaps in executable coverage of their runtime security risks, while evolving model capabilities, harnesses, tools, and threats motivate benchmark evolution. We introduce EvoRiskBench , an evolving benchmark or-ganized around the EP–Path–EF framework, which links an initial risk entry point to an one-hop technical effect through an agent-mediated risk path. The framework defines nine entry-point categories and five effect categories; a 20-participant study supports their interpretability and classification consistency on representative cases. Guided by this framework, an automated end-to-end work-flow constructs and executes risk cases in isolated environments and indepen-dently verifies outcomes using runtime traces and environment states. The bench-mark provides a reproducible dataset of 450 adversarial tasks across six scenar-ios. We evaluate nine Model × Harness configurations spanning three models (GPT-5.6 Sol, DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813, and Claude Opus 5) and three harnesses (Claude Code, Codex, and OpenClaw). Our results reveal substantial vulnerabil-ities across systems. The most vulnerable configuration, Codex with DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813, reaches a 68.44% attack success rate (ASR), indicating that config-uration of workspace agent is insufficient to ensure secure autonomous execution. ASR varies more across models than harnesses, and harness differences depend on the model. The benchmark cases and evaluation platform will be released after completion of artifact safety and reproducibility checks. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that execute multi-step tasks through interaction with tools and external environments (Wang et al., 2024). We re-fer to workspace agent as stateful Model × Harness systems that pursue user-delegated tasks through successive observations and tool-mediated actions, accessing or modifying external re-sources and state . This formulation subsumes API-based tool agents and GUI-based computer-use agents(Rawles et al., 2025) as interface-specific instances of LLM agents, while emphasizing persis-tent state, orchestration, permissions, and heterogeneous resources. The harness manages context, execution state, tool interfaces, permissions, and orchestration, mediating how model-selected ac-tions are executed in the environment. Unlike response-only conversational systems, workspace agents can turn model outputs into operations with direct technical effects on external resources. We therefore focus on runtime security risks arising from agent execution, assessing both observable execution behavior and its direct, verifiable technical effects. Agent-security evaluation has broadened to cover explicitly harmful multi-step tasks (An-driushchenko et al., 2025), indirect prompt injection (IPI) in tool-using environments (Debenedetti et al., 2024), and trajectory-dependent harms that emerge through accumulated context and tool-mediated execution (Feng et al., 2026a). However, executable coverage of workspace-agent runtime risks remains incomplete: risks entering through agent communication and subagent delegation have only partial or no identified coverage in the compared benchmarks, while coverage of technical effects such as resource exhaustion is uneven (Table 1). Moreover, testing a technical effect through one entry interface does not establish coverage of other interfaces or execution paths that may lead to the same effect. A fixed set of test cases may also become less representative as model capabili-ties, harnesses, tools, and threats evolve. Beyond coverage, reliable evaluation requires monitoring the workspace during execution to corroborate technical effects that may not be fully captured by agent-visible trajectories or final-state checks. To address these limitations, we introduce EvoRiskBench , an evolving benchmark for runtime se-curity risks in workspace agents. We focus on indirectly induced runtime risks, where adversarial content enters through workspace resources and interfaces, rather than through explicitly malicious user requests. As illustrated in Figure 1, the framework represents each risk case as [ EP → Path → EF ]where EP (Risk Entry Point) identifies the initial interface through which risk enters under the case’s starting conditions, the Path (Agent-mediated Risk Path) captures its propagation through observ-able actions and state changes; and EF (Risk Effect) specifies the one-hop technical consequence targeted by the case. Inspired by the Layered Attack Surface Model (LASM) (Chu, 2026), we identify nine runtime risk entry-point categories . Drawing on prior benchmark taxonomies (Li et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026), we define five technical-effect categories spanning confidentiality, integrity, and availability(CIA) (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2004). Agent-mediated paths connect these categories to executable case construction and outcome verification. Risk Path generation and independent system-level evaluation. Execution traces and replay-verification feedback guide adversarial refinement and subsequent case generation. The same workflow supports extending the benchmark with additional validated cases as model capabilities, harnesses, tools, and threats evolve. Using this workflow, we construct 450 executable adver-sarial tasks across six scenarios , which form a shared, frozen case set for reproducible cross-configuration evaluation. 2 RELATED WORK 

Workspace Agents. Tool-using LLM agents interact with external environments through APIs and other tools (Schick et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023; 2025), while computer-use agents extend this interaction to graphical and operating-system interfaces (Rawles et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024). These interface-specific agents can also be viewed as instances of a broader class of workspace agents: systems that pursue user-delegated tasks in evolving environments containing files, code, tools, external services, and persistent state (Zhou et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Jimenez et al., 2024). Their behavior is jointly determined by the model and its harness, which manages con-text, execution state, tool interfaces, permissions, and orchestration. The same model may therefore exhibit different runtime behavior across harnesses. Following this perspective, EvoRiskBench eval-uates complete, stateful Model × Harness configurations, examining risks arising as agents access or modify external resources during task execution. 

Agent Security Benchmarks and Runtime Risk Coverage. AgentHarm evaluates explicitly harmful task completion (Andriushchenko et al., 2025), while ToolEmu examines agent risks in sandboxed tool-use settings (Ruan et al., 2024). AgentDojo, InjecAgent, and WASP study indirect prompt injection (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024; Evtimov et al., 2025). ASB further covers attacks such as memory poisoning and backdoors (Zhang et al., 2025a). AgentHazard and AgentS4D emphasize complete execution trajectories and runtime risks across the agent lifecycle (Feng et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026), while AgentCanary evaluates agent security in executable environments (Li et al., 2026b). OpenAgentSafety further provides a broader framework for eval-uating real-world agent safety (Vijayvargiya et al., 2026). Related analyses examine layered attack surfaces and inconsistencies across benchmark risk taxonomies (Chu, 2026; Li et al., 2026a). These efforts provide complementary threat models and ways of organizing risk. EvoRiskBench focuses on risks indirectly induced through untrusted workspace content, using an EP–Path–EF represen-tation to connect entry points, agent-mediated execution paths, and direct technical effects. Since one entry point can lead to different effects and the same effect can arise through different paths, representing both dimensions supports executable case construction and coverage analysis. Table 1 compares executable coverage across risk entry points and risk effects. 

Evolving Benchmarks and Adversarial Case Construction. Fixed test suites may become less representative as agent capabilities, harnesses, and tools evolve, motivating evaluation methods that continually extend and refine cases (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). Dynamic risk as-sessment studies iterative improvement of offensive agents (Wei et al., 2025), while GPT-Red uses scalable self-play to discover prompt-injection attacks across models and harnesses (Wallace et al., 2026). Vera further automates risk discovery, executable case construction, and evidence-grounded verification, using execution feedback to guide subsequent exploration (Feng et al., 2026b). Build-ing on this direction, EvoRiskBench organizes adversarial case construction through EP–Path–EF constraints. Execution feedback collected under a fixed Model × Harness configuration guides case refinement, while validated cases and observed failure modes inform subsequent generation. This workflow supports continued expansion with additional validated cases. Case refinement occurs during construction, while formal evaluation uses a frozen case set across Model × Harness config-urations.

3.1 RUNTIME RISK FORMULATION 

Workspace-agent risks cannot be fully characterized by attack techniques alone. The same adversar-ial technique may enter an agent through different runtime interfaces and propagate through different execution paths, while the same entry interface may lead to different technical consequences de-pending on agent behavior, permissions, and environmental context(Liu et al., 2024). Therefore, we represent runtime risks through two primary dimensions: a risk entry point (EP ) and a risk effect 

(EF ). The relationship between these two dimensions is instantiated through an agent-mediated risk path during execution. Formally, let 

EP = {ep 1, . . . , ep 9}, EF = {ef 1, . . . , ef 5} (1) denote the sets of risk entry points and risk effects, respectively. Rather than treating an (ep i, ef j )

pair as a static label, EvoRiskBench associates each pair with an execution path that describes how adversarial influence is propagated and realized by the agent. For a model M , harness H, and executable case c, the agent produces an observable trajectory: 

τM,H (c) = ( o0, a 1, o 1, . . . , a T , o T , y ), (2) where at denotes a tool-mediated action, ot denotes the corresponding agent-visible observation, and y denotes the final response generated by the agent. In addition to agent-visible trajectories, EvoRiskBench collects external runtime evidence EM,H (c),including system-level events and independently obtained environment states. These evidence sources jointly characterize the realized agent-mediated path:

Threat Model. We consider only IPI attacks in which an attacker can place or manipulate mali-cious content in an untrusted workspace resource exposed to the agent through a risk entry point, while the user provides a benign task. Beyond controlling this untrusted content, the attacker can-not modify trusted system or developer instructions, model parameters, harness implementations, environment configurations, or verification components; directly invoke tools or perform the target operation; or forge execution evidence. Direct compromise of the model or harness is outside our scope. Risk Entry Point. We identify nine risk entry points by locating where adversarial influence enters or becomes actionable during workspace-agent execution, following the layered attack-surface per-spective (Chu, 2026). As summarized in Table 2, these entry points span four broad attack surfaces: 

memory and retrieval , tool and execution , multi-agent collaboration , and the agent ecosystem . This organization supports systematic coverage of runtime risk beyond specific attack techniques. 

Risk Effect. Prior agent-security benchmarks characterize harmful outcomes by their impact on data, system state, and resources (Li et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026). Building on these taxonomies and the CIA security properties, we define five effect categories, summarized in Table 3. Each category captures a one-hop technical effect : an observable and verifiable consequence directly produced by agent execution, regardless of how many actions are required to realize it. For example, in a Data Tampering case ( ef 3), overwriting a configuration file constitutes the directly verifiable effect; any subsequent service failure is a downstream consequence and does not alter the pre-specified effect label. 

Taxonomy Assessment. Twenty participants independently classified 11 representative scenarios—six for EPs and five for EFs—without seeing the intended labels. Majority labels matched the intended categories in all 11 scenarios, with Fleiss’ κ = 0 .904 for EPs and κ = 0 .723 

for EFs. These results support interpretability and consistent application on the tested scenarios. Appendix A.1 reports the study protocol, subjective ratings, and qualitative feedback. 3.2 EXECUTABLE CASE GENERATION & R EFINEMENT

Evidence-Guided Refinement. Candidates that fail the replay criterion are not discarded imme-diately. Finding 1: Even high-performing workspace agents exhibit non-negligible vulnerability to IPI.

5 CONCLUSION , LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

We present EvoRiskBench, an execution-grounded benchmark for runtime security risks in workspace agents under IPI. It characterizes risk through nine entry points and five verifiable effects, instantiated by 450 executable benchmark cases. Its execution-feedback-driven construction and verification workflow supports case refinement and versioned expansion, while cross-configuration 9Preprint evaluation uses a frozen, shared case set. Across 4,050 runs over nine Model × Harness configura-tions, EvoRiskBench reports 37.46% ASR and 60.44% TSR. ASR varies more across models than harnesses, and harness differences depend on the model. ASRs differ across entry points and effects, while task utility varies across configurations. These results support joint security-utility evaluation of complete workspace agents and motivate runtime defenses. Our evaluation focuses on IPI attacks and uses sandboxed tools, services, and state that approximate but may not fully capture the complexity of production workspaces. Future work will broaden the threat model beyond IPI and evaluate EvoRiskBench with more realistic external services, hetero-geneous workspaces, and long-running scenarios. AI U SE STATEMENT

A.1 AN USER STUDY 

Participants and protocol. We recruited 20 volunteer participants with backgrounds spanning AI/LLM/agent security, cybersecurity, software application security, red teaming, and agent-system development. Participants reported an average familiarity of 4.10 /5 with Agent/LLM systems and 

4.00 /5 with Agent/LLM security. The study was designed to evaluate both the subjective quality and practical interpretability of the proposed runtime risk taxonomy. 

Subjective assessment. Participants evaluated the clarity and distinctiveness of each taxonomy category on a five-point Likert scale. Risk Entry Points received an average clarity score of 4.24 

and distinctiveness score of 3.91 , while Risk Effects achieved 4.51 and 4.18 , respectively. At the taxonomy level, 100% of participants rated the coverage of the Risk Entry Point taxonomy as at least “agree”, and 95% did so for the Risk Effect taxonomy. 

Blind classification. To assess whether the taxonomy can be applied consistently in practice, par-ticipants independently classified 11 representative agent-security scenarios without being shown the intended labels. Six cases evaluated Risk Entry Points and five evaluated Risk Effects. The majority label matched the intended category in all 11 cases, with particularly high agreement for the Risk Entry Point cases. Across all 11 cases, we obtain an overall Fleiss’ κ = 0 .841 , indicating high agreement when the entry-point and effect labels are treated as a joint label space. Separate analyses yield κ = 0 .904 for Risk Entry Points and κ = 0 .723 for Risk Effects. 

Qualitative feedback. We additionally asked participants to identify categories whose boundaries were difficult to interpret and to report potentially missing risks. Feedback was concentrated on a small number of boundary cases, particularly communication- and delegation-related entry points, while most participants reported no obvious missing entry-point or effect category. Run isolation. Each run receives a disposable Windows process-isolated container, with its workspace, session, and configuration initialized from the fixed image and case specification.

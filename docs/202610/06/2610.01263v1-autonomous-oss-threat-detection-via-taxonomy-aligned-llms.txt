Abstract 

Open source software (OSS) ecosystems face growing threats from sophisticated supply chain attacks including typosquatting, dependency confusion, Trojan Source obfuscation, malicious build injection, and CI/CD pipeline poisoning. Existing detection approaches rely on signature-based tools and rule-based systems that struggle to generalize across attack variants and emerging threat pat-terns. In this paper we propose a taxonomy-aligned large language model framework for automated detection and classification of OSS supply chain threats. We introduce a structured AV-xxx threat taxonomy covering five attack categories and construct a curated dataset of 999 verified real-world OSS supply chain incidents sourced from GitHub Security Advisories, CISA alerts, and security research reports spanning 2018 to 2026. Using taxonomy-aligned prompt engineering with GPT-4, our framework achieves 97.0% multi-class classification accuracy and 97.0% macro F1 score across all five threat categories. Comparative evaluation against five traditional machine learning baselines, one zero-shot open source LLM, and two fine-tuned neural models reveals a surprising finding: fine-tuned Llama 3.1 8B (70.5%) and SecRoBERTa (77.5%) both underperform simple TF-IDF classifiers (82.3%), while Mistral 7B without taxonomy alignment achieves only 65.7%. These results confirm that taxonomy-aligned prompting rather than model scale, domain pretrain-ing, or fine-tuning is the critical factor enabling high classification accuracy. Our dataset and code are publicly available to support reproducible supply chain security research. 

Keywords: software supply chain security, large language models, threat classification, OSS secu-rity, taxonomy-aligned prompting, dependency confusion, typosquatting, pipeline poisoning 

# 1 Introduction 

Open source software (OSS) underpins critical infrastructure, commercial platforms, and research ecosys-tems worldwide. The Linux Foundation estimates that open source components comprise 70 to 90 percent of modern application codebases [19], making the OSS supply chain a high-value target for adversaries seeking broad downstream impact through a single compromise point. Supply chain attacks against OSS ecosystems have escalated dramatically in recent years. Attackers exploit the implicit trust relationships within developer communities by injecting malicious packages into public registries, manipulating build scripts, and poisoning CI/CD pipelines. High-profile incidents including the XZ Utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094), the tj-actions/changed-files compromise (CVE-2025-30066) [5] affecting over 23,000 repositories, and coordinated campaigns by threat actors such as 1arXiv:2610.01263v1 [cs.CR] 1 Oct 2026 TeamPCP and Lazarus Group demonstrate that OSS supply chain attacks have evolved from isolated incidents into systematic campaigns operating at ecosystem scale [6] [7]. Despite growing awareness, a critical gap remains between academic threat taxonomies and opera-tional detection capabilities. Existing tools rely on rule-based or signature-driven approaches that lack the semantic understanding needed to generalize across attack variants. Furthermore, no systematic framework exists for classifying diverse OSS supply chain threats according to a unified taxonomy that spans the full attack surface from package registry manipulation to pipeline poisoning. In this paper we propose a taxonomy-aligned large language model (LLM) framework for automated classification of OSS supply chain threats. Our approach introduces a structured AV-xxx threat taxonomy covering five attack categories: typosquatting (AV-200), Trojan Source obfuscation (AV-300), malicious build injection (AV-400), pipeline poisoning (AV-410), and dependency confusion (AV-509). Using taxonomy-aligned prompt engineering with GPT-4, we demonstrate that LLMs can accurately classify supply chain threats with significantly higher accuracy than traditional machine learning approaches, fine-tuned neural models, and zero-shot open source LLMs. Our main contributions are as follows: • We construct a curated dataset of 999 verified real-world OSS supply chain incidents sourced from GitHub Security Advisories, CISA alerts, NHS England Digital advisories, and security research reports spanning 2018 to 2026, covering five attack categories across multiple package ecosystems including npm, PyPI, RubyGems, Cargo, Maven, and Go modules. • We propose a taxonomy-aligned prompt engineering strategy that guides GPT-4 to classify OSS supply chain threats with 97.0% accuracy and 97.0% macro F1 score, outperforming the strongest traditional baseline (TF-IDF + Logistic Regression at 82.3%) by 14.7 percentage points. • We conduct a comprehensive comparative evaluation against five traditional machine learning baselines, one zero-shot open source LLM (Mistral 7B), and two fine-tuned neural models (Llama 3.1 8B and SecRoBERTa), revealing that fine-tuned models surprisingly underperform simple TF-IDF classifiers, confirming that taxonomy-aligned prompting rather than model scale or fine-tuning is the critical performance factor. • We release our dataset, fine-tuning scripts, and experimental code publicly to support reproducible research in automated OSS supply chain threat detection. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents our dataset construction methodology. Section 4 describes our taxonomy-aligned LLM clas-sification framework. Section 5 presents experimental results and comparative evaluation. Section 7 concludes the paper. 

# 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 OSS Supply Chain Security 

Attacks on software supply chains have escalated dramatically in recent years, targeting open source ecosystems through dependency manipulation, malicious package injection, and CI/CD pipeline com-promise. High-impact incidents including the event-stream npm compromise [1], the PyTorch-2nightly dependency hijack [2], the SolarWinds SUNBURST backdoor [3], and the XZ Utils back-door CVE-2024-3094 [4] have demonstrated the severity and scale of modern supply chain threats. Sonatype’s annual State of the Software Supply Chain report documented over 1.2 million cumulative malicious packages across npm, PyPI, Maven Central, and NuGet as of 2026 [6]. Existing mitiga-tions include Software Composition Analysis (SCA) tools, trusted build pipelines, and package signing mechanisms. However, these approaches are largely reactive and signature-based, lacking the semantic understanding needed to generalize across novel attack variants and emerging threat patterns. 

## 2.2 Threat Taxonomies for Supply Chain Attacks 

Several taxonomies have been proposed for classifying software supply chain attacks. Ohm et al. [8] introduced a taxonomy of malicious open source packages based on attack techniques and payload types. OWASP’s Software Component Analysis framework provides guidance on dependency security but does not address the full spectrum of supply chain attack vectors. MITRE ATT&CK includes supply chain compromise as technique T1195 but does not provide fine-grained classification across specific OSS attack vectors such as typosquatting, Trojan Source, and dependency confusion. Our work intro-duces a structured AV-xxx taxonomy that specifically addresses five distinct OSS supply chain attack vectors, enabling systematic automated classification across the full attack surface of modern package ecosystems. 

## 2.3 Large Language Models for Security Analysis 

Large language models have demonstrated significant potential across a range of security tasks. Prior work has explored LLMs for vulnerability description and CVE analysis [11], exploit summariza-tion [12], and code security review [13]. Domain-specific models including SecurityBERT [14] have been fine-tuned on security corpora to improve performance on threat classification tasks. Recent work has also explored fine-tuning general-purpose LLMs such as Llama [26] on domain-specific datasets for security classification. However, the effectiveness of fine-tuning versus structured prompting for OSS supply chain threat classification remains unexplored. Our work addresses this gap by demonstrating that taxonomy-aligned prompt engineering with GPT-4 achieves 97.0% classification accuracy, signifi-cantly outperforming both fine-tuned neural models and zero-shot open source LLMs. 

## 2.4 Automated Threat Detection in OSS Ecosystems 

Automated detection of malicious packages in OSS registries has attracted increasing research attention. Duan et al. [9] proposed static analysis techniques for detecting malicious npm packages. Luo et al. [10] applied machine learning to identify malicious PyPI packages using metadata and behavioral features. Commercial tools including Socket.dev [18], Phylum [23], and Sonatype Lifecycle [6] provide real-time malicious package detection using behavioral heuristics and provenance analysis. However, these tools focus on binary malicious versus benign classification and do not provide fine-grained threat categoriza-tion aligned with a structured attack taxonomy. Our approach extends the state of the art by providing multi-class classification across five semantically distinct attack vectors, enabling more actionable threat intelligence for security teams responding to OSS supply chain incidents. Despite progress in automated package analysis and LLM-based security tools, no prior work has (1) constructed a verified dataset of this scale specifically for OSS supply chain threat classification, (2) pro-posed a structured taxonomy spanning five distinct attack vectors across multiple package ecosystems, 3or (3) systematically evaluated taxonomy-aligned LLM prompting against traditional ML baselines, zero-shot open source LLMs, and fine-tuned neural models for this task. Our work addresses all three gaps. 

# 3 OSS Threat Dataset 

We construct and publicly release a curated dataset of 999 verified real-world OSS supply chain security incidents to support reproducible research and scalable benchmarking in supply chain threat detection. Each entry represents a known or recently disclosed supply chain attack sourced from authoritative se-curity databases and research reports. The dataset is structured according to our AV-xxx threat taxonomy and covers incidents from 2018 to 2026 across multiple package ecosystems. 

## 3.1 Data Collection Methodology 

Incidents were collected from the following primary sources: • GitHub Security Advisories [20] (github.com/advisories) — Official GHSA advisories docu-menting verified malicious packages with assigned advisory identifiers • GitHub Security Lab (securitylab.github.com) — GHSL advisories documenting Poisoned Pipeline Execution vulnerabilities in open source CI/CD workflows • Government and Regulatory Sources — CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, NHS England Digital cyber alerts, and NVD CVE database entries • Security Research Organizations — Sonatype, Socket.dev, Checkmarx [21], ReversingLabs, Datadog Security Labs [24], Snyk [22], JFrog, Unit42, Arctic Wolf, and Orca Security incident reports • Official Vendor Disclosures — PyPI security blog, npm security advisories, and affected project security announcements Each incident was manually verified against its source to confirm that the described attack actually occurred, that the source URL directly documents the specific incident, and that the assigned AV label correctly reflects the primary attack vector. Entries with unverifiable sources or generic attack pattern descriptions not tied to specific documented incidents were excluded. 

## 3.2 Schema and Taxonomy 

Each incident record contains the following fields: • id : Unique incident identifier (e.g., gh101 )• title : Concise incident title including specific package or software name where applicable • description : Plain-language description of the incident including attack mechanism, affected ecosystem, and observed impact • label : Ground truth classification using the AV-xxx taxonomy 4• source : Verified source URL from an authoritative security database or research report • predicted label : LLM-generated classification label for evaluation Our AV-xxx taxonomy covers five primary OSS supply chain attack categories: • AV-200 — Typosquatting : Malicious packages that mimic legitimate package names through character substitution, transposition, or namespace impersonation • AV-300 — Trojan Source : Attacks exploiting Unicode bidirectional control characters or homo-glyphs to create source code that executes differently from its visual representation [17] • AV-400 — Malicious Builds : Packages containing malicious code executed during installation, build, or import processes through lifecycle hooks such as postinstall and setup.py 

• AV-410 — Pipeline Poisoning : Attacks that compromise CI/CD pipeline execution including GitHub Actions workflow injection, poisoned pipeline execution (PPE), and build system com-promise • AV-509 — Dependency Confusion : Attacks exploiting package manager resolution priority to cause build systems to install malicious public packages instead of intended private internal de-pendencies [16] [16]

# 4 Methodology 

Our taxonomy-aligned LLM framework for OSS supply chain threat classification consists of three components: a structured threat taxonomy, a prompt engineering strategy for LLM-based classification, and a comparative evaluation protocol. 

## 4.1 Threat Taxonomy Design 

We define a structured AV-xxx taxonomy covering five primary OSS supply chain attack vectors. The taxonomy was designed based on analysis of real-world incident patterns documented in GitHub Se-curity Advisories, CISA alerts, and security research reports. Each category represents a semantically distinct attack mechanism with different technical characteristics and detection requirements. The five categories are defined as follows. AV-200 (Typosquatting) covers attacks where mali-cious packages mimic legitimate package names through character-level manipulation. AV-300 (Trojan Source) covers attacks exploiting Unicode bidirectional control characters to create code that executes differently from its visual representation. AV-400 (Malicious Builds) covers packages containing ma-licious code executed during installation or build processes through lifecycle hooks. AV-410 (Pipeline Poisoning) covers attacks that compromise CI/CD pipeline execution including GitHub Actions work-flow injection and poisoned pipeline execution. AV-509 (Dependency Confusion) covers attacks exploit-ing package manager resolution priority to install malicious public packages instead of private internal dependencies.

64.2 Taxonomy-Aligned Prompt Engineering 

Our classification approach uses instruction-tuned LLMs guided by a taxonomy-aligned system prompt that provides explicit definitions of all five attack categories. The prompt is structured as follows: 

You are an expert in open source software supply chain security. Classify the following security incident into exactly one of these categories: AV-200 (Typosquatting), AV-300 (Tro-jan Source), AV-400 (Malicious Builds), AV-410 (Pipeline Poisoning), AV-509 (Dependency Confusion). Respond with only the category label. 

Each incident is represented as the concatenation of its title and description fields, providing suf-ficient contextual information for accurate classification. We use GPT-4 [25] as our primary clas-sification model with temperature set to zero to ensure deterministic outputs. GPT-4 classification was performed on all 999 entries using a single-pass evaluation with predicted labels recorded in the 

predicted label field for comparison against ground truth. The taxonomy-aligned prompt serves two functions. First it constrains the model output space to ex-actly five valid labels, eliminating invalid or ambiguous responses. Second it provides explicit semantic definitions of each category, enabling the model to disambiguate between related attack vectors such as AV-400 and AV-410 that share surface-level characteristics but differ in their target component (package versus pipeline). 

## 4.3 Comparative Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate our taxonomy-aligned LLM approach against five traditional machine learning baselines using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the full 999-entry dataset. The baselines are: • Random Classifier : Assigns labels uniformly at random across five classes • Majority Class : Always predicts the most frequent class in the training set • TF-IDF + Na¨ ıve Bayes : Bag-of-words TF-IDF features with Multinomial Na¨ ıve Bayes classifier using unigram and bigram features • TF-IDF + Logistic Regression : TF-IDF features with sublinear term frequency scaling and ℓ2-regularized Logistic Regression (C=1.0) • TF-IDF + LinearSVC : TF-IDF features with a Linear Support Vector Classifier (C=1.0) All TF-IDF classifiers use a vocabulary of 10,000 features with English stop word removal and unigram-bigram tokenization. Text features are constructed by concatenating incident title and descrip-tion fields. We additionally evaluate Mistral 7B [15] as a zero-shot open source LLM baseline using the same taxonomy-aligned prompt via the Ollama inference framework on a stratified sample of 200 incidents (40 per class). This comparison isolates the contribution of the GPT-4 model capability versus the taxonomy-aligned prompting strategy. To further evaluate the utility of our dataset for training specialized models, we conduct fine-tuning experiments on two neural models using an 80/20 stratified train/test split (799 training, 200 test en-tries). First, we fine-tune Llama 3.1 8B [26] using QLoRA 4-bit quantization with LoRA rank 8, α=16, 7dropout=0.1, learning rate 2e-5, and 5 training epochs. Second, we fine-tune SecRoBERTa [27] — a RoBERTa model pretrained on cybersecurity corpora — using standard sequence classification fine-tuning with batch size 16, learning rate 2e-5, weight decay 0.01, and early stopping with patience 2. Both models use maximum sequence length 256 tokens with title and description concatenated as input features. Primary evaluation metrics are classification accuracy and macro-averaged F1 score across all five categories. Macro F1 is reported to give equal weight to each category regardless of support size.

We evaluate our taxonomy-aligned LLM framework on 999 verified OSS supply chain incidents using classification accuracy, macro F1 score, and per-class performance metrics. We compare against five tra-ditional machine learning baselines, one zero-shot open source LLM, and two fine-tuned neural models to assess the contribution of our taxonomy-aligned prompting strategy.

We employ GPT-4 via the OpenAI API with temperature set to zero for deterministic outputs. Classifica-tion is performed using taxonomy-aligned prompting as described in Section 4. The predicted label

All TF-IDF classifiers use 10,000 features with unigram-bigram tokenization and English stop word removal. 5.5.1 Key Insight 

These four examples illustrate a consistent pattern: traditional TF-IDF classifiers make errors when at-tack descriptions contain keywords strongly associated with a related but incorrect category. Taxonomy-aligned LLM prompting resolves these ambiguities by reasoning about the primary attack mechanism — whether the attack exploits package naming (AV-200), code obfuscation (AV-300), installation hooks (AV-400), pipeline components (AV-410), or registry resolution (AV-509) — rather than surface vocab-ulary patterns. 

## 5.6 Discussion 

Our results demonstrate four key findings. First, traditional TF-IDF classifiers achieve reasonable per-formance at 82.3% but fail to reliably disambiguate semantically related attack categories particularly at the AV-400 and AV-410 boundary. Second, fine-tuned neural models including SecRoBERTa (77.5%) and Llama 3.1 8B (70.5%) surprisingly underperform TF-IDF classifiers, likely due to overfitting on the relatively small training set of 799 examples, suggesting that dataset scale remains a critical bottleneck for LLM fine-tuning in specialized security domains. Third, open source LLMs without taxonomy align-ment (Mistral 7B at 65.7%) underperform even simple TF-IDF classifiers, confirming that taxonomy-aligned prompting rather than raw LLM capability is the primary performance driver. Fourth, our taxonomy-aligned GPT-4 approach achieves 97.0% accuracy by leveraging pre-existing security knowl-edge through structured prompting without requiring any training data, establishing a strong benchmark for automated OSS supply chain threat classification. One limitation worth noting is the near-perfect baseline classification of AV-300 Trojan Source (197/198), which suggests that Trojan Source incidents in our dataset share highly distinctive textual patterns involving Unicode terminology. Future dataset construction should incorporate more linguisti-cally diverse AV-300 examples to better evaluate classifier generalization on this category. 

## 5.7 Threats to Validity 

Internal validity. Our dataset was constructed through manual curation and verification against primary sources. While intra-rater reliability testing achieved Cohen’s kappa of 1.00 on a 50-entry stratified sample, the full dataset was labeled by a single annotator and may reflect individual interpretation biases at ambiguous category boundaries particularly between AV-400 and AV-410. 

External validity. Our evaluation uses retrospective incident reports from public security databases. Classification performance on novel real-time attack patterns not yet documented in security advisories may differ from reported results. 

Construct validity. Our taxonomy-aligned GPT-4 evaluation uses temperature zero for determinis-tic outputs. Results may vary with different GPT-4 model versions as the API is updated over time. We mitigate this concern by providing our exact prompt, dataset, fine-tuning scripts, and evaluation code publicly for reproducibility verification. 13 6 Limitations and Future Work 

## 6.1 Limitations 

Our study has several limitations that inform directions for future work. First, our GPT-4-based ap-proach depends on a proprietary model that may change over time, potentially affecting reproducibility. Second, while our dataset of 999 incidents provides strong coverage of known attack patterns, it may not fully capture emerging attack vectors that have not yet been publicly documented. Third, our eval-uation focuses on textual classification of documented incidents rather than real-time detection of novel attacks in live package registries. Fourth, our fine-tuning experiments demonstrate that 799 training ex-amples are insufficient for competitive LLM fine-tuning in this domain, suggesting that larger datasets are needed to unlock the full potential of open source models for OSS threat classification. 

## 6.2 Future Work 

Several directions are identified for future research. 

Larger dataset for fine-tuning. Our experiments show that fine-tuned Llama 3.1 8B (70.5%) and SecRoBERTa (77.5%) both underperform simple TF-IDF classifiers (82.3%), likely due to overfitting on the small training set of 799 examples. Future work will expand the dataset to 5,000 or more ver-ified incidents to enable competitive fine-tuning of open source models that match or exceed GPT-4 performance without dependence on proprietary APIs. 

Real-time registry monitoring. Future work will extend our framework from retrospective incident classification to real-time monitoring of package registry submissions, enabling proactive detection of malicious packages before they accumulate downloads. 

Dataset expansion and continuous updating. The OSS supply chain threat landscape evolves rapidly with new attack campaigns documented regularly. Future work will develop automated pipelines for continuous dataset expansion from GitHub Security Advisories, CISA alerts, and security research publications. 

Multi-modal threat analysis. Beyond textual incident descriptions, future work will incorporate package metadata, behavioral signals, and code-level features to improve classification of borderline cases particularly at the AV-400 and AV-410 boundary. 

Cross-ecosystem generalization. Our current dataset covers incidents from npm, PyPI, RubyGems, Cargo, Maven, Go, and Docker ecosystems. Future work will evaluate whether taxonomy-aligned prompting generalizes effectively across ecosystems with distinct package naming conventions and at-tack patterns. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We presented a taxonomy-aligned large language model framework for automated detection and classifi-cation of open source software supply chain threats. Our approach introduces a structured AV-xxx threat taxonomy covering five attack categories and constructs a curated dataset of 999 verified real-world OSS supply chain incidents spanning 2018 to 2026 across multiple package ecosystems and threat actor groups. Experimental evaluation across eight methods — including five traditional ML baselines, one zero-shot open source LLM, and two fine-tuned neural models — demonstrates that our taxonomy-aligned 14 GPT-4 approach achieves 97.0% classification accuracy and 97.0% macro F1 score, outperforming all evaluated methods by a substantial margin. A surprising finding emerges from our fine-tuning experi-ments: both SecRoBERTa (77.5%) and Llama 3.1 8B (70.5%) fine-tuned on our dataset underperform simple TF-IDF classifiers (82.3%), while Mistral 7B without taxonomy alignment achieves only 65.7%. These results confirm that taxonomy-aligned prompting rather than model scale, domain pretraining, or fine-tuning is the critical factor enabling high classification accuracy, and suggest that dataset scale remains a key bottleneck for open source LLM fine-tuning in specialized security domains. Error analysis reveals that classification challenges concentrate at the boundary between semanti-cally related attack categories particularly AV-400 (Malicious Builds) and AV-410 (Pipeline Poisoning), demonstrating why contextual LLM reasoning is necessary beyond simple keyword-based approaches. Our dataset, fine-tuning scripts, and experimental code are publicly available to support reproducible research in automated OSS supply chain threat detection. We believe taxonomy-aligned LLM frame-works represent a promising direction for scalable, interpretable security analysis of the OSS supply chain threat landscape.
